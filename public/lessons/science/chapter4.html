Introduction to Magnets

Magnets have been used historically to find directions at sea. Natural magnets called lodestones and artificial magnets are common today in items like pencil boxes, toys, and magnetic dusters.

Magnets come in different shapes such as bar, ring, U-shaped, and others.

Magnets were essential for sailors to navigate when stars were obscured by storms.

Magnetic & Non-Magnetic Materials

Materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt are magnetic and attracted by magnets. Others like wood, plastic, and rubber are non-magnetic and not attracted.

Try gathering objects made of various materials, predict if they will stick to a magnet, and then test your predictions.

Poles of Magnet & Finding Directions

Magnets have two poles: North and South. Iron filings concentrate at the poles. Magnets always have poles in pairs—even the smallest piece.

A freely suspended magnet aligns itself along the north-south direction due to Earth's magnetic field.

A magnetic compass uses this property to find directions with a freely rotating magnetized needle.

Attraction & Repulsion Between Magnets

Unlike poles (North-South) attract; like poles (North-North, South-South) repel. Iron bars get attracted at both ends when a magnet is brought close.

A magnet can be identified by observing repulsion between poles.

Compass needles deflect when a magnet's pole is brought close, showing magnetic interaction.

Magnetic Compass & Fun Activities

Construct a magnetic compass by magnetizing a needle, balancing it on cork, and floating it on water.

Magnets can move objects without contact, enabling fun activities like making magnetic garlands, moving steel balls in mazes, and lifting paperclips from water.

Always treat magnets carefully; store them properly with opposite poles facing together and place soft iron pieces across ends.
